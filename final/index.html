Empty Houses, Familiar Neighborhoods

Syracuse’s vacant properties continue to cluster in areas shaped by historic neglect

Idrissa Ballo

Photo: Austin Neill / Unsplash


On blocks across Syracuse’s Near Westside, Southside, and parts of the city’s North and East sides, boarded-up homes and empty lots sit alongside occupied houses. The citywide perception of vacancy as a major issue does not match the actual distribution of vacant properties which Syracuse data shows exist mainly in areas that have faced prolonged economic decline and abandonment.

An analysis of the City of Syracuse’s Vacant Properties dataset shows that empty homes are not randomly distributed. The population groups themselves into particular residential areas which developed through historical processes of redlining and industrial collapse and population decrease and inadequate public funding. The observed patterns have raised questions about Syracuse housing operations and which groups will experience the negative impacts when properties transform into mostly investment properties.

Vacancy Concentrates Where Investment Has Been Scarce

The Vacant Properties dataset, published through the City of Syracuse open data portal, lists thousands of parcels identified by the city as vacant. The data shows different patterns when researchers arrange the information according to geographical areas. Neighborhoods such as the Near Westside, Southside, Brighton, Elmwood, and Washington Square appear repeatedly in the data, while more affluent areas of the city show far fewer vacant properties.

Cluster's in specific areas indicates that vacancy results from more than personal carelessness and single market problems. Specific characteristics of particular neighborhoods establish conditions which result in abandonment of certain areas. A combination of aging residential buildings and low real estate prices and restricted funding options creates obstacles for property owners to invest which leads to increasing vacancy rates that build up throughout multiple years.

These findings align with broader research on Syracuse’s housing market. A city-commissioned housing study found that “much of the housing in Syracuse has been neglected for decades,”contributing to stagnant property values and widespread deferred maintenance. The long duration of empty properties makes it difficult for property owners and municipal authorities to build permanent residential areas in previously inhabited neighborhoods which remain unoccupied.

Housing as a Commodity, Not a Right

Sociologist Gretchen W. Purser, an associate professor at Syracuse University’s Maxwell School, said chronic vacancy reflects deeper problems in how housing operates in the United States.

“I think chronic vacancy just speaks to the fact that housing isn't built for housing, like that we don't provide people like the basic right to a home. Rather, housing is a commodity that is bought and sold.”

Purser pointed to the contradiction at the heart of the vacancy crisis: empty homes existing alongside severe housing insecurity
.
“The absurdity is that we have a high rate of housing vacancy, and a high rate of homelessness. So that itself speaks to power dynamics between landlords and tenants and just the general like lack of social and economic rights that we as human beings have in a capitalist society.”

In Syracuse, those power dynamics are visible on the map. Neighborhoods with less political influence and fewer economic resources are more likely to absorb the consequences of vacancy, while areas with stronger markets remain relatively insulated. .

The Neighborhood Impact of Concentrated Vacancy

Vacancy affects more than just the buildings listed in the dataset. The concentration of abandoned houses in particular blocks of the neighborhood produces distinct living experiences for residents who choose to stay in their community. Research on housing shows that when vacant properties exist in high numbers within a specific area it creates problems which affect neighborhood stability and drives away investors while making the area more dangerous.

What the Data Shows and What It Can’t

The Vacant Properties dataset reveals that Syracuse experienced ongoing patterns of inequality which resulted in vacant properties throughout the city. The areas with the most vacant properties contain neighborhoods which have received minimal funding throughout multiple decades.

The data includes particular limits though which prevent its application. It does not explain why each individual property is vacant, how long it has been empty, or whether an owner intends to rehabilitate or sell it. The dataset fails to show how residents experience blight and insecurity and housing instability in their daily lives

Why This Matters Now

As Syracuse deabtes redevelopment ideas, housing affordability, and enforcment priorities, it is critical to understand where and why vacancies exist. Vacancy is more than just the lack of people in buildings. It demonstrates how regulations and market factors allow certain communities to collapse while others continue to attract investment.

Researchers suggest that filling vacancies requires more than isolated adjustments. It necessitates a larger view of housing as a public benefit, as well as a commitment to historically underserved communities.
